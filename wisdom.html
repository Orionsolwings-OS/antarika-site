Discover Antarika Dhyaan

✦

Antarika Dhyaan is a journey from the outer world to the inner self, inviting us to pause, observe, and become aware of what is happening within. It is not about escaping from life, but about understanding life with greater clarity and awareness. Through simple and consistent practice, we learn to observe our thoughts, emotions, and reactions without being carried away by them. As observation deepens, the mind gradually becomes calmer, clearer, and more balanced.

Dhyaan creates a space between what happens and how we respond, allowing us to live with greater awareness. In stillness, we begin to listen to the subtle silence within and discover a deeper sense of presence. Antarika Dhyaan encourages simplicity, self-understanding, inner balance, and conscious living.

The journey does not begin somewhere outside; it begins within ourselves. There is no need to force the mind or search for something extraordinary, simply pause, observe, and allow awareness to unfold naturally. With patience and sincerity, stillness becomes a doorway to deeper understanding.

Sadhana — The Journey Within

Sadhana is a conscious journey of turning inward. It is not merely a technique or a temporary practice, but a way of cultivating awareness in how we think, feel, speak, act, and experience life. Through meditation, breath awareness, silence, mantra, and disciplined observation, Sadhana gradually brings the mind from restlessness toward greater clarity, steadiness and inner awareness.

In the traditional understanding of Vak, expression is described through four levels — Para, Pashyanti, Madhyama, and Vaikhari. These four levels provide a subtle way of contemplating how expression moves from its most unmanifest state into thought and finally into spoken words. For meditation, they can also be contemplated as a journey from outer expression toward the deeper source of awareness.

Para

The most subtle and unmanifest level. The word Para means "supreme" or "beyond." It points toward the source before thought, intention, or expression takes a definite form. Para cannot be understood merely through intellectual analysis or heard through the physical senses. In Sadhana, it can be approached through profound stillness and silence, where one becomes attentive to that which exists before the movement of thought.

Pashyanti

"That which sees." It represents a subtler level where meaning or intention is perceived before it becomes clearly formulated as words. It can be contemplated as the inner perception or intuitive movement that arises before verbal expression. Through meditation, one learns to notice these subtle movements without immediately naming, judging, or reacting to them.

Madhyama

"Middle." The internal level of expression, where thoughts, intentions, and inner speech take shape. Before a word is spoken, it often exists within as an idea or mental expression. Meditation allows us to observe this inner dialogue directly. Thoughts arise, remain for a moment, and pass away, while awareness remains present. Through this observation, we gradually learn that we can witness the movement of the mind without being carried away by every thought.

Vaikhari

The expressed and audible form of speech. It is the level through which thoughts and intentions become words and enter the outer world. Vaikhari reminds us that speech itself can become part of Sadhana. The words we choose, the way we communicate, and the intention behind our expression can reflect our inner state. Conscious speech therefore becomes an extension of meditation into everyday life.

The movement from Vaikhari to Madhyama, from Madhyama to Pashyanti, and from Pashyanti toward Para can be contemplated as an inward journey. We begin by observing what we express, then become aware of the thoughts behind our expression, then become sensitive to subtler intentions and perceptions, and finally turn toward the silence from which all these movements arise.

At Antarika, meditation is approached not as an escape from life, but as an opportunity to understand life more consciously. Sadhana encourages us to pause, observe, and become aware of what is happening within us. With regular practice, this awareness can gradually extend into our relationships, work, communication, decisions, and everyday actions.

The essence of Sadhana is not to chase extraordinary experiences. It is to develop a sincere relationship with awareness itself. To sit quietly, observe without judgement, return to the present moment, and allow understanding to unfold naturally.

Antarika — a journey from outer expression to inner awareness, from activity to stillness, and from observation to understanding.

Self-Discipline

Purifying oneself through self-observation is not an easy process. It requires continuous practice, patience, perseverance, and self-discipline. Every seeker must discover their own inner truth through their own sincere effort and experience; no one else can do this journey for them. Therefore, meditation is best approached by those who are willing to follow the discipline that is an essential part of the practice and to make a sincere and dedicated effort.

One of the great sages of India, Adi Shankaracharya, emphasized the importance of continuous and dedicated practice. Without consistent practice and deep contemplation, it is not easy to gain a clear understanding of the true nature of the Self. Therefore, a seeker should meditate deeply and repeatedly over a long period, with patience and sincerity. This practice is not only for personal peace and happiness, but also for the well-being, happiness, and welfare of all.

External objects, situations, and experiences may give us temporary happiness, but they cannot provide lasting joy. Yet, we often keep searching for happiness outside ourselves, constantly moving from one experience to another. In this continuous search, we gradually lose our energy, peace, and inner balance. This can leave us feeling dissatisfied, disappointed, anxious, irritated, and angry. When we begin to understand that lasting happiness cannot be found in external things, but is discovered through inner awareness and stillness, our journey naturally turns inward. Self-discipline, self-observation, meditation, and continuous practice become the foundation of this inward journey.
